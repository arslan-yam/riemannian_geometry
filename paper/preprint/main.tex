\title{Certified Distance Queries on Terrain Surfaces in Microseconds}

\author{Arslan Yamaletdinov}
\affiliation{\institution{Affiliation}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
Shortest-path distances on terrain surfaces underlie nearest-neighbour, range and route queries in geographic
information systems, robotics and planning. Exact geodesic algorithms take seconds per pair on large terrains, and
the distance oracles proposed for terrains answer faster but approximately, without saying how wrong a given
answer is. We present the Certified Cut Index (\cci), to our knowledge the first implemented terrain distance index whose
every answer carries a two-sided bound that adapts to the query and is guaranteed within $1\pm\eps$: a query returns an estimate together with $\LB\le d\le\UB$, where $d$
is the exact surface distance, $(1-\eps)d\le\LB$ and $\UB\le(1+\eps)d$, proved in exact arithmetic and validated in
floating point against exact solvers. The interval adapts to the query and is usually far narrower than $\eps$, so
kNN and range queries are decided from the intervals and only the undecided candidates are computed exactly. Every
vertex stores exact geodesic distances to portals sampled on the straight cut lines of a kd hierarchy: a window
lemma bounds where a shortest path can cross a cut, an ellipse lemma makes every label an exact geodesic on a small
sub-mesh, and the 1-Lipschitz label rows give the lower bound. A capped variant answers with a certificate or
exactly, refines only pairs close to a capped cut, and on terrains of bounded slope and detour stores
$O\big((k+\eps^{-1}\log k)\,n\log n\big)$ labels plus $O(\eps^{-2})$ near-table entries per vertex. On six terrains
with 0.5M faces, \cci answers in 0.5--2\,$\mu$s on one core with a mean error of 0.001--0.03\% in 0.36--0.66\,GB;
EAR-Oracle needs 0.4--1\,s and 333\,GB, and hub labels on the graph of DGG, the most accurate competitor,
60--94\,$\mu$s and 29--48\,GB, both without a per-query bound. All 8.5 million answers we checked lie in their
intervals, except two where the exact reference solver itself is wrong. The index also answers queries between
arbitrary surface points, handles obstacles, and gives an upper bound on weighted terrains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A terrain is usually stored as a triangulated irregular network (TIN): a triangulation of sampled
points whose projection to the plane is a planar triangulation. Many queries on terrains are distance
queries. A nearest-neighbour query asks for the closest objects to a location, a range query asks for all
objects within a given distance, and both are routine in geographic information systems, in path
planning for vehicles and robots, and in the analysis of disasters and military operations. The distance
in these applications is the length of the shortest path on the surface, not the Euclidean or
the projected one, which can be off by tens of percent in mountains.

Computing a single surface distance exactly is expensive. The classical algorithm of Mitchell, Mount and
Papadimitriou (MMP)~\cite{mitchell1987discrete} and its practical successors~\cite{surazhsky2005fast,
xin2009improving,qin2016fast} propagate a wavefront of windows over the mesh, and one query costs
milliseconds on a few thousand vertices and seconds on a hundred thousand. For applications that ask many
queries, the database community has proposed distance oracles for terrains: SE-Oracle~\cite{wei2017distance},
EAR-Oracle~\cite{huang2023ear}, RC-Oracle~\cite{yan2024rc} and UP-Oracle~\cite{yan2025up}. They replace the
surface by a Steiner graph or a point cloud and index it with well-separated pair decompositions or spanners.
Their guarantees are a priori factors: $1\pm\eps$ against the exact distance between points of interest
(SE-Oracle), $1+\eps$ with an additional additive term for arbitrary points (EAR-Oracle), or a factor against a
point-cloud graph rather than the surface (RC-Oracle). We measured mean errors of 0.8--3.5\% and maximum errors
up to 25\% against exact geodesics, with indexes of hundreds of megabytes already at 10k vertices and of
333\,GB at 0.5M faces.
The most accurate practical method we found is not a terrain oracle at all: DGG~\cite{adikusuma2020fast} builds in
minutes a sparse graph whose shortest paths approximate geodesics to a relative error of order $\eps$ when the
vertices are evenly spread, and with $\eps=10^{-4}$ its mean error is about 0.004\%. But DGG answers every query
with a Dijkstra search on that graph, 0.12--0.51\,s per pair even when the search stops at the target at 0.5M
faces, and returns no bound for the individual answer. It is a better surface model, not an index. Putting a road-network index on
its graph does not close the gap: contraction hierarchies stall on its high degree, and hub labels answer in
60--94\,$\mu$s but need 29--48\,GB and 13--42\,h at 0.5M faces (Section~\ref{sec:server}).

What none of these methods provides is a statement about a single answer. A query operator needs more than a good
average. A kNN query compares the distances of many candidates, a range query compares each with a radius, and a
planner may need to know that a route is no longer than a limit. With a point estimate of unknown error, every such
decision is a guess near its boundary; with an interval $\LB\le d\le\UB$ that is guaranteed to contain the exact
surface distance, most decisions can be made from the intervals alone, and the few that cannot are exactly the ones
worth computing exactly (Figure~\ref{fig:knn}). An oracle whose answer $\hat d$ is a path length with a guarantee
$\hat d\le(1+\eps)d$ implies the interval $[\hat d/(1+\eps),\hat d]$, but only if the guarantee holds against the
exact distance without an additive term, which only SE-Oracle's does, for points of interest, and its width is
always $\eps$. To decide a query, the
interval must be narrow when it can be and known to be narrow.

Road networks faced the problem of fast distance queries and solved it with labelling indexes.
H2H~\cite{ouyang2018hierarchy} and hierarchical cut labelling~\cite{farhan2023hierarchical} let every vertex store
its distances to the separator vertices of its ancestors, so that a query is a minimum over one separator. The same
layout is behind Thorup's approximate oracles for planar graphs~\cite{thorup2004compact}, which keep distances to
$O(1/\eps)$ portals on shortest-path separators, and it has been carried to weighted polyhedral surfaces through
Steiner graphs~\cite{aleksandrov2010algorithms,djidjev2011approximate} and to mesh geodesics with compressed
distance functions~\cite{gotsman2022compressing}. These indexes return a single value. On a terrain that value
either rests on a discretised surface or comes with no bound that the query can check.

We propose the Certified Cut Index (\cci), a labelling index for terrain surfaces whose every answer is a certified
interval. We keep the label layout and the minimum over one cut, and change three things. First, the separators are
straight axis-parallel cut lines of a kd hierarchy~\cite{bentley1975multidimensional} over the chart of the terrain,
not shortest paths. The portal argument of Thorup's oracles needs a shortest-path separator, so we replace it with a
window lemma that bounds where a shortest path can cross a cut and what crossing far away costs; portals are then
sampled at an arc gap $g$ near the region and at geometrically growing gaps away from it. Second, every label is an
exact geodesic distance on the continuous surface, computed on a small sub-mesh, rather than a graph, Steiner-graph
or fitted value. Third, a straight cut makes the label rows 1-Lipschitz in arc length along the cut, and this gives a
lower bound next to the upper bound. The resulting interval adapts to the query: at $\eps=0.2$ its median width is
0.36\% uncapped and 2\% with a cap of 64 portals per cut, far below $\eps$. In summary
(Theorems~\ref{thm:main}--\ref{thm:a2a}):

\begin{headline}[informal]
Let $T$ be a terrain, $\eps\in(0,1)$ and $g>0$, and let the labels be exact geodesic distances, as we compute
them on unweighted terrains. In exact arithmetic, for every pair of vertices the index returns $\LB\le d\le\UB$
with $(1-\eps)d\le\LB$ and $\UB\le(1+\eps)d$, in time linear in the depth of the hierarchy and the number of
portals of one cut. With at most $k$ portals per cut inside the region, every answer satisfies
$\UB\le(1+\eps)\LB$ or is computed exactly, and on terrains of bounded slope, detour and vertex density without
obstacles the index stores $O\big((k+\eps^{-1}\log k)\,n\log n\big)$ labels and $O(\eps^{-2})$ exact near-table entries
per vertex for a fixed gap $g$. Built with tails at $\eps/(2+\eps)$, it refines only pairs close to a capped cut, with
a local sweep. Between arbitrary surface points, $\UB\le(1+2\eps)\LB$.
\end{headline}

Our main contribution is, to our knowledge, the first implemented terrain distance index whose every answer
between two vertices or two surface points carries an interval that contains the exact surface distance, lies within
$1\pm\eps$ of it and adapts to the query, with $\UB/\LB\le1+\eps$ or an exact answer. Lower and upper bounds on
terrain distances exist (Section~\ref{sec:related}), but either with no bound on their width or, when implied by a
$(1+\eps)$ oracle, with the fixed width $\eps$. Three ingredients make it work, and each is needed: a window lemma that bounds
where a shortest path can cross a straight cut, so a cut is sampled rather than stored; an ellipse lemma that makes
every label an exact geodesic computed on a small sub-mesh; and a lower bound read from the 1-Lipschitz label rows
(Section~\ref{sec:index}). Around it we contribute:
(i) its guarantees: the $(1\pm\eps)$ interval, and for the capped variant certify-or-refine with near-linear labels
and a refine-locality theorem under stated geometric assumptions (Sections~\ref{sec:capped} and~\ref{sec:a2a});
(ii) certified kNN and range queries that decide from the intervals and refine exactly only what they cannot decide
(Section~\ref{sec:engineering});
(iii) engineering that keeps the bounds sound while making the index fast and compact: hybrid exact sweeps,
closed-loop 8/16-bit label coding and a parallel build with bit-identical labels (Section~\ref{sec:engineering});
(iv) an extension to terrains with obstacles, with the same certificate, and to weighted terrains, with an
upper bound only; and
(v) an evaluation against every terrain oracle with available code, a mesh-geodesic index and a learned baseline,
run on the same machine and the same queries, on terrains from 800 vertices to 5M faces
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

Table~\ref{tab:delta} summarises how \cci differs from the indexes closest to it; the paragraphs below give the
details.

\paragraph{Exact and approximate geodesics.}
MMP~\cite{mitchell1987discrete} computes single-source geodesics exactly by propagating windows of constant
unfolding over the mesh; Chen and Han~\cite{chen1990shortest} gave an $O(n^2)$ algorithm, and the practical
implementations of Surazhsky et al.~\cite{surazhsky2005fast}, ICH~\cite{xin2009improving} and the
triangle-oriented VTP~\cite{qin2016fast} made exact geodesics routine. We use the first and the last to
compute labels. Fast marching~\cite{kimmel1998computing} and the heat method~\cite{crane2013geodesics}
approximate distances by solving PDEs on the mesh. The saddle vertex graph~\cite{ying2013saddle} and its
successor DGG~\cite{adikusuma2020fast} precompute a sparse graph whose shortest paths approximate geodesics
very well, but a query still runs Dijkstra on a graph with more edges than the mesh, and takes milliseconds.

\paragraph{Terrain distance oracles in databases.}
SE-Oracle~\cite{wei2017distance} places Steiner points on the edges and builds a compressed partition tree
with well-separated node pairs, answering POI-to-POI queries within a factor $1\pm\eps$ of the exact distance.
EAR-Oracle~\cite{huang2023ear} extends this to arbitrary points with highway nodes on an adaptive grid; its
answer is at most $(1+\eps)(d+2\delta)$, with an additive $\delta$ from the discretisation. RC-Oracle~\cite{yan2024rc}
targets point clouds, with a factor against the point-cloud graph, and UP-Oracle~\cite{yan2025up} terrains that
change, both for a set of POIs. All of them compute their distances on a discretised surface and return a single
value with an a priori guarantee, but no bound for the individual answer.
EAR-Oracle is the closest in structure: it partitions the chart by a uniform grid and stores, for the vertices
on box boundaries, approximate distances to the Steiner points inside the box, and answers a query by Dijkstra
on these highway nodes. It has one level, approximate labels and an additive error term. Per-query lower and
upper bounds on terrain distances are not new: Kaul et al.~\cite{kaul2013finding,kaul2015new} derive both,
with a fixed ratio of about 0.9, by a search per pair, and earlier surface kNN
methods~\cite{deng2006surface,shahabi2008indexing} filter candidates with Euclidean lower bounds and network
upper bounds before an exact refinement. We follow the same filter-and-refine pattern, but our bounds are read
from an index in a microsecond and their ratio is controlled by $\eps$.

\paragraph{Oracles on polyhedral surfaces with theory.}
Aleksandrov et al.~\cite{aleksandrov2005determining,aleksandrov2010algorithms} and Djidjev and
Sommer~\cite{djidjev2011approximate} built $(1+\eps)$ oracles for weighted polyhedral surfaces from a
Steiner discretisation with geometrically placed points and shortest-path separators of the resulting graph.
Djidjev and Sommer achieve near-linear space,
$O(n\eps^{-3/2}\log^2\frac{n}{\eps}\log^2\frac{1}{\eps})$, and query time
$O(\eps^{-1}\log\frac1\eps + \log\log n)$. Near-linear space is therefore known in theory, and our
uncapped index is asymptotically larger. As far as we know, neither line of work has an implementation,
their constants include the Steiner density and the smallest face angle, and neither returns a per-query
interval. Our geometric portal spacing is a relative of their geometrically placed Steiner points, applied
along a cut instead of around vertices. In polygonal domains with obstacles, Thorup~\cite{thorup2007compact}
and van der Laan et al.~\cite{vanderlaan2026approximate} use the same recursion with shortest-path separators
of the free space and $O(1/\eps)$ anchor points per vertex and separator.

\paragraph{Precomputed geodesics in geometry processing.}
The closest work to ours is SEP by Gotsman and Hormann~\cite{gotsman2022compressing}. It bisects a mesh
recursively with separator curves, stores for every vertex its distances to the curves of its ancestors,
restricted to each ancestor's patch, and answers a query by minimising over the shared curves; its conclusion
suggests axis-parallel cuts as an alternative. Its distances are low-degree polynomial fits, the separators
are sampled on a fixed grid, and pairs in the same leaf use the Euclidean distance. Its error bound holds only
if every fit is accurate everywhere, which the method does not enforce, and it reports mean errors of
0.1--0.4\% on meshes of up to 5k vertices. Because its distances are restricted to patches, every ancestor curve
has to be scanned; our labels are distances on the whole surface, so the deepest separating cut suffices. In short, \cci keeps SEP's layout and makes four changes: straight cuts sampled where the window lemma says a path
can cross, instead of separator curves sampled on a fixed grid; exact geodesics on the whole surface instead of
per-patch fits; one cut per query instead of all ancestors; and a lower bound, so that every answer is checked
rather than trusted. We reimplemented SEP over exact VTP sweeps (its code is not public) and compare against it in
Section~\ref{sec:server}.
Landmark methods store distances to a few sample points: Xin et al.~\cite{xin2012constant} interpolate them
in constant time without a bound, and Burghard and Klein~\cite{burghard2015simple} return the triangle-inequality
lower and upper bounds through the landmarks, whose relative width is not bounded; we evaluate this baseline in
Section~\ref{sec:server}.

\paragraph{Distance labels and portals on graphs.}
Planar graphs have small separators~\cite{lipton1979separator}, and the approximate oracles of
Klein~\cite{klein2002preprocessing}, Thorup~\cite{thorup2004compact} and Kawarabayashi et
al.~\cite{kawarabayashi2011linear} store distances to portals on shortest-path separators; Thorup's
$\eps$-covers keep $O(1/\eps)$ portals per vertex and separator. Exact distance labels on planar graphs need
$\Theta(\sqrt n)$ entries per vertex in the worst case~\cite{gavoille2004distance}, which is the graph analogue
of our $n^{1.5}$ total. On road networks, hub labels~\cite{abraham2011hub}, pruned landmark
labelling~\cite{akiba2013fast}, H2H~\cite{ouyang2018hierarchy} and hierarchical cut
labelling~\cite{farhan2023hierarchical} answer exact queries in microseconds; the last two store, for every
vertex, distances to the vertices of its ancestor separators or cuts. ALT~\cite{goldberg2005computing} derives
lower bounds from the triangle inequality through landmarks to guide a search. \cci transfers the layout of
H2H to a continuous surface: cut lines instead of vertex separators, sampled portals instead of whole bags,
exact geodesic labels, and a certified interval instead of an exact minimum. Its lower bound does not go
through a landmark; it uses that a shortest path must cross the cut, and that the labels change by at most the
arc length between neighbouring portals.

\paragraph{Learned distances.}
NeuroGF~\cite{zhang2023neurogf} fits a neural field to one mesh and answers a query with one forward
pass, and GeGnn~\cite{pang2023learning} learns a geodesic embedding with a graph neural network. Neither has an
error bound, and a fixed capacity has to cover the whole surface.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

A terrain $T$ is a triangulated height field: a triangulation of points $(x_v,y_v,z_v)$ whose projection to
$(x,y)$ is a planar triangulation of a domain $U\subset\mathbb R^2$, the chart. We write $n$ for the number
of vertices. Each face $f$ carries a cost $w_f \ge w_{\min} > 0$; an unweighted terrain has $w\equiv 1$. The
cost $L(\gamma)$ of a curve $\gamma$ on $T$ is $\sum_f w_f$ times its 3-D length inside $f$, and $d(p,q)$ is
the infimum over curves from $p$ to $q$. Shortest paths exist by compactness. We assume that $T$ is connected,
and a query with $s=t$ returns $\LB=\UB=0$. For a point $p$ of $T$ we write
$p$ also for its chart position and $P$ for the point in space.

Two lower bounds are used throughout. Put $\lambda := w_{\min}$. On a face, the metric pulled back to the
chart is $w_f^2(I + \nabla z\nabla z^\top) \succeq w_f^2 I$, and 3-D length dominates the chord, so for every
curve $\gamma$ from $p$ to $q$
\begin{equation}
L(\gamma) \;\ge\; \lambda\, L_{\mathrm{chart}}(\gamma) \;\ge\; \lambda\,|p-q|,
\qquad L(\gamma) \;\ge\; \lambda\,|P-Q|.
\label{eq:lower}
\end{equation}
The index has two parameters: the accuracy $\eps\in(0,1)$ and a portal gap $g>0$, an arc length on the
surface. They define the near radius $R := g/(\eps\lambda)$. We consider vertex-to-vertex (V2V) queries and
arbitrary-point (A2A) queries, and measure the relative error of an estimate $\hat d$ as $|\hat d/d - 1|$.

\subsection{Cut hierarchy and windows}

The index is built on a kd hierarchy of the vertices. A region $M$ is a set of vertices with chart bounding
box $B_M$. If the diagonal of $B_M$ is at most $R$, the region is a leaf. Otherwise we cut it along the axis
$a$ of the larger extent at a value $v$ strictly between the two middle member coordinates (if these are equal,
between the nearest pair of distinct ones), chosen so that no vertex of $T$ has $a$-coordinate $v$. The cut line is $\ell_C=\{x_a=v\}$, and the children are the
members on its two sides. We write $I_C=\ell_C\cap B_M$.

A shortest path between two members of $M$ on opposite sides of the cut must cross $\ell_C$ (by continuity of
its chart projection), but it may leave the box first. The window bounds how far. Pick a hub $h\in M$ near
the centre of the box and let $\mu_C = \max_{u\in M}\tilde U(h,u)$, where $\tilde U$ is the mesh-graph
distance, an upper bound of $d$ because a graph path is a curve on $T$.
\begin{lemma}[window]
\label{lem:window}
Let $s,t\in M$ lie on opposite sides of $\ell_C$, let $\gamma$ be a shortest path between them and
$x^\ast\in\gamma\cap\ell_C$. Then $d(s,t)\ge 2\lambda\,\dist(x^\ast,I_C)$, and $\dist(x^\ast,I_C)\le\mu_C/\lambda$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:lower}, $d(s,t)\ge\lambda(|s-x^\ast|+|x^\ast-t|)\ge 2\lambda\dist(x^\ast,B_M)$, and for a point
of $\ell_C$ the nearest point of $B_M$ lies on $I_C$. On the other side, $d(s,t)\le d(s,h)+d(h,t)\le 2\mu_C$.
\end{proof}
The window $W_C=\{x\in\ell_C:\dist(x,I_C)\le\mu_C/\lambda\}$ therefore contains every crossing that matters.
Its intersection with the surface is a union of segments, the runs, which we compute exactly from the faces
the line crosses. On each run the lifted cut is a polyline, and we measure its cost $A(\cdot,\cdot)$ along it: arc
length weighted by the face costs, which is plain arc length on an unweighted terrain. The cut is a curve on $T$,
so $d(p,q)\le A(p,q)$ for two of its points.

\subsection{Portals and labels}

The portals $P_C$ of a cut are points of the runs, sorted along the line, including the ends of every run and
of $I_C$. For two consecutive portals of a run with arc distance $A_j$ we require
\begin{equation}
A_j \le g \text{ if the interval lies in } I_C, \qquad A_j\le\max(g,\,2\eps\lambda c_j)\text{ otherwise},
\label{eq:gaps}
\end{equation}
where $c_j$ is the chart distance from the interval to $I_C$. Inside the box the portals are dense; outside
it the gap grows linearly with the offset, which Lemma~\ref{lem:window} allows because a path crossing at
offset $c$ is at least $2\lambda c$ long. The tails therefore hold only $O(\eps^{-1}\log(\mu_C/\lambda R))$
portals.

Every member $u$ of $M$ stores the exact distances $\ell(u,p)=d(u,p)$ to all portals $p\in P_C$, and the
rows of all cuts on the path from $u$'s leaf to the root form its label. In addition, every vertex $u$ stores a
near table with the exact distances to the vertices $N_R(u)$ within chart distance $R$.

\paragraph{Exact labels on small sub-meshes.}
A label is a geodesic distance on the whole surface, but it can be computed on a small part of it.
\begin{lemma}[ellipse]
\label{lem:ellipse}
If $d(x,u)\le D$, every point of a shortest path from $x$ to $u$ lies within chart distance $D/(2\lambda)$ of
the segment $[x,u]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:lower}, a point $z$ of the path has $|z-x|+|z-u|\le D/\lambda$, so it lies in the ellipse with foci
$x,u$ and major axis $D/\lambda$, whose points are within its semi-minor axis
$\frac12\sqrt{(D/\lambda)^2-|x-u|^2}\le D/(2\lambda)$ of the segment.
\end{proof}
With $D$ a graph upper bound through the hub, all label geodesics of a cut stay inside the bounding box of
$M\cup P_C$ dilated by $D/(2\lambda)$. We take the faces that meet this box, insert the portals as vertices
(on their faces' planes, so the surface does not change), and run one exact single-source sweep per portal
on this sub-mesh. A geodesic of $T$ inside the sub-mesh is a curve of the sub-mesh and conversely, so the
sub-mesh distances are exact. The near tables are computed the same way, from one sweep per vertex on its
own small sub-mesh.

\subsection{Query and certificate}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Query $(s,t)$}
\label{alg:query}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{$|s-t|\le R$} \Return $d(s,t)$ from the near table of $s$ \EndIf
\State $C\gets$ the deepest cut whose region contains $s$ and $t$
\State $f_j\gets\ell(s,p_j)+\ell(t,p_j)$ for every portal $p_j\in P_C$
\State $\UB\gets\min_j f_j$
\State $\LB\gets\max\big(\lambda|S-T|,\ \min_j[\tfrac12(f_j+f_{j+1})-A_j]\big)$ \Comment{consecutive portals of a run}
\State $\hat d\gets$ the interpolated minimum of $f$, clamped into $[\LB,\UB]$
\State \Return $(\hat d,\LB,\UB)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:query} answers a query. If $s$ and $t$ are not near, they lie in different leaves, hence on
opposite sides of the deepest cut $C$ whose region contains both. Every $f_j$ is the length of a path through
$p_j$, so $\UB=\min_j f_j\ge d(s,t)$. The lower bound uses that a label row is 1-Lipschitz along the cut in
arc length: $|\ell(u,p)-\ell(u,q)|\le A(p,q)$.

\begin{lemma}[sandwich]
\label{lem:sandwich}
$\LB\le d(s,t)\le\UB$ for every pair.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Near pairs are exact. Otherwise let $x^\ast$ be a crossing of a shortest path, which lies on a run between
consecutive portals $p_j,p_{j+1}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:window}, and let $a=A(p_j,x^\ast)$. The cut itself is a
curve, so $d(x^\ast,p_j)\le a$, and
$f_j\le d(s,x^\ast)+a+a+d(x^\ast,t)=d(s,t)+2a$. Likewise $f_{j+1}\le d(s,t)+2(A_j-a)$. Adding gives
$\frac12(f_j+f_{j+1})-A_j\le d(s,t)$, and the chord term is a lower bound by~\eqref{eq:lower}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:main}
If the labels and near tables are exact, then in exact arithmetic, for every pair of vertices,
$(1-\eps)\,d(s,t)\le\LB\le d(s,t)\le\UB\le(1+\eps)\,d(s,t)$,
with equality when $|s-t|\le R$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For a non-near pair, $d:=d(s,t)\ge\lambda|s-t|>\lambda R=g/\eps$ by~\eqref{eq:lower}. From the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich}, $\UB\le\min(f_j,f_{j+1})\le d+A_j$. If the crossing interval lies in $I_C$,
$A_j\le g<\eps d$. Otherwise its offset satisfies $d\ge 2\lambda c_j$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:window}, and
\eqref{eq:gaps} gives $A_j\le\max(g,2\eps\lambda c_j)\le\eps d$. The lower bound follows the same way: an
interval term is at least $d-A_j$, and a tail interval with $A_j>\eps d$ has both portals at offset more than
$d/(2\lambda)$, where every $f$ is at least $2\lambda c_j>d$. The full proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.
\end{proof}

The certificate is not only a worst-case statement. $\UB/\LB$ is known for every answer, and it is usually
far below $1+\eps$: the median interval width is 0.7--2\% for vertex pairs in our experiments. The returned estimate is the
interpolated minimum of the label sum, which in our experiments is 3--4 orders of magnitude more accurate
than $\eps$.

\paragraph{What is certified in floating point.}
The theorems assume exact labels and arithmetic. We compute labels in double precision, store them as float32
rounded up, and lower every stored label by the matching margin of $2^{-22}$ before it enters a lower bound. (The runs
reported here lowered the finished bound by that margin instead, which falls short when an arc gap is large against
the bound; the strict counts below show that this never mattered, and our code now uses the label margin.) a refined pair returns
$d(1\pm10^{-12})$ around one sweep ($10^{-9}$ between arbitrary points). These margins cover the storage rounding,
not the rounding of the solvers, which we have not analysed, so we validate rather than prove: every answer in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} is checked against an independent double-precision MMP sweep, and we call it
certified and correct if $\LB\le d(1+10^{-9})$, $d\le\UB(1+10^{-9})$, and $\UB\le(1+\eps)\LB$ or it was refined.
The tolerance is far above what we observe. Without it, of the 1.88 million answers of the runs where we count this
(Section~\ref{sec:server}: build repeats, adversarial terrains, small terrains), 35,403 lie outside the raw
interval, all by at most $1.2\cdot10^{-13}$ relative; with the default lossless near tables 153, by at most
$4.5\cdot10^{-16}$, two units in the last place. These are double-precision roundings where a bound coincides with
the distance, as for a near-table entry or a ratio-coded one that decodes to it.

\paragraph{Cross-checking the solvers.}
The labels are only as exact as the solvers, and on degenerate meshes the solvers fail. On the clustered terrain
of Table~\ref{tab:adversarial}, with corner angles below $10^{-5}$, below which Kirsanov's MMP is documented to be
unreliable, MMP misses vertices and returns distances up to $7\cdot10^{-4}$ too long. Both solvers fail in this
direction, by missing a path, so the build can sweep a chosen fraction of the cuts with both MMP and VTP, keep the
smaller value at each target and count the disagreements. On Gunnison10k with every cut checked, the two solvers
disagree on 2 of 6.76 million labels, by at most $3\cdot10^{-8}$ relative. On a clustered variant of Gunnison10k
built the same way, they disagree in 344 of 83,744 sweeps, on 642 labels, by up to 8.3\%, and one of them leaves
36 labels unreached. Keeping the smaller value, all 1,000 random pairs of the capped index lie in their intervals.
That reference is MMP itself; the cluster run of Table~\ref{tab:adversarial} checks against VTP. Checking every cut doubles the sweeps. Checking one cut in 20 costs about 5\%, and at this rate of
failure it would still flag the mesh.

\subsection{Capped portals and certify-or-refine}
\label{sec:capped}

The top cuts are long and have $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ portals each, and every vertex stores them, so the labels
grow like $n^{1.5}$ in total. The capped variant puts at most $k$ portals inside the box of each cut: its gap
is $g_C=\max(g,A(I_C)/k)$, and its tails step by $\max(g_C,2\eps\lambda c)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich} does not
use the gap, so the interval stays sound. The query accepts an answer with $\UB\le(1+\eps)\LB$; otherwise it
computes the distance exactly, with one sweep on the faces meeting $\mathrm{bbox}(s,t)$ dilated by $\UB/(2\lambda)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:ellipse}). For a cut $C$ let $r_C$ be the number of runs of its window, $\sigma_C$ the largest cost
per unit of chart length along it, and $\kappa_C=\mu_C/(\lambda|I_C|)$ the length of the window relative to the
cut's span in the box.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:capped}
Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, every answer of the capped index satisfies $\UB\le(1+\eps)\LB$ or
is exact. A cut has at most
$k+O(r_C)+O\big(\frac{\sigma_C}{\eps\lambda}(1+\log^+(\eps k\kappa_C))\big)$ portals, and the index stores
$\sum_u\sum_{C\ni u}|P_C|$ labels and $\sum_u|N_R(u)|$ near-table entries, where $N_R(u)$ are the vertices within
chart distance $R$ of $u$. If $\sigma_C/\lambda$, $\kappa_C$ and $r_C$ are bounded, as on terrains of bounded
slope and detour without obstacles, the labels hold $O\big((k+\eps^{-1}\log k)\,n\log n\big)$ entries.
\end{theorem}
Obstacles can add many runs, and clustered vertices large near tables; the theorem counts both. The near tables
hold $O(\rho g^2/(\eps\lambda)^2)$ entries per vertex for vertex density $\rho$, independent of $n$ and of the cap.
The acceptance test is stricter than the worst case of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, which gives only
$\UB/\LB\le(1+\eps)/(1-\eps)$. Building the tails with $\delta=\eps/(2+\eps)$ in place of $\eps$, so that
$(1+\delta)/(1-\delta)=1+\eps$, closes this gap, and then the refines are local. Let
$w_C=g_C/(\delta\lambda)$ and $\beta=\max_C\sigma_C/(\lambda k\delta)$.
\begin{theorem}[refine locality]
\label{thm:locality}
Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, let the capped index have tail gaps $\max(g_C,2\delta\lambda c)$.
A non-near pair answered at cut $C$ is refined only if $d(s,t)<g_C/\delta$. Then $s$ and $t$ lie within chart
distance $w_C$ of the cut line and of each other, and the refine sweeps the faces meeting a chart box of side less
than $(2+\delta)w_C$. If every chart square of side $r\ge g$ holds at most $\rho_{\max}r^2$ vertices and every box
of the hierarchy at least $\rho_{\min}$ times its area, at most $\pi(\rho_{\max}/\rho_{\min})^2\beta^3n^2$ vertex
pairs are refined at capped cuts; at the other cuts, $g_C=g$, so only pairs within chart distance $(2+\eps)R$.
\end{theorem}
The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}) shows $\UB\le d+m$ and $\LB\ge d-m$ with $m=\max(g_C,\delta d)$. Only capped
cuts, where $g_C>g$, refine pairs with $d\ge g/\delta$; the refinable fraction falls like $k^{-3}$; and a refine
sweeps a neighbourhood of the cut of width $O(w_C)$, not the terrain. Theorem~\ref{thm:capped} holds with $\delta$
in place of $\eps$, so the tails hold at most $2+\eps$ times as many portals. The count is loose, because a pair close to a capped cut is
usually accepted anyway: $\LB$ also takes the chord $\lambda|S-T|$, which is within $1+\eps$ of $d$ unless the path
detours. On Gunnison10k with $\eps=0.1$ and cap 8, $\delta$ tails make the index 2\% larger, and all 14 refined
pairs out of 1,000 have $d\le4.8\,g_C$, against the bound $21\,g_C$. Building the tails with $\eps$ refines the same
14 pairs, so our other runs use $\eps$ tails, and we measure how often the refine runs (Section~\ref{sec:server}):
with cap 64, for no random pair.

\subsection{Arbitrary surface points}
\label{sec:a2a}

For a point $p$ on face $f$ with corners $a_i$ and a point $q$ on face $f'$ with corners $b_j$, the straight
segment inside a face is a path, so $d(p,a_i)\le w_f|P-A_i|$. The vertex certificates and the triangle
inequality give
\begin{align*}
\UB(p,q) &= \min_{i,j}\ w_f|P-A_i|+\UB(a_i,b_j)+w_{f'}|Q-B_j|,\\
\LB(p,q) &= \max\Big(\lambda|P-Q|,\ \max_{i,j}\ \LB(a_i,b_j)-w_f|P-A_i|-w_{f'}|Q-B_j|\Big).
\end{align*}
Pairs within $R$, and pairs whose interval is wider than $1+2\eps$, are computed exactly by one sweep between
the two points on an ellipse sub-mesh.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:a2a}
Every A2A answer satisfies $\LB\le d(p,q)\le\UB\le(1+2\eps)\LB$.
\end{theorem}

Table~\ref{tab:complexity} summarises the costs. The build runs one exact sweep per portal, so it costs

smaller than that of our uncapped index; our contribution is an implemented index that certifies every
answer, with constants we measure.

\section{Engineering}
\label{sec:engineering}

\paragraph{Hybrid exact sweeps.}
We use two exact solvers: Kirsanov's implementation of MMP~\cite{surazhsky2005fast} and
VTP~\cite{qin2016fast}. VTP is 2.6--4.1 times faster per full sweep, but its vertex queue does not make a
popped vertex final, so it has no exact early stop. MMP processes windows in order of their minimum
distance, and once that minimum exceeds the current value at every target no later window can improve any of
them, so it stops early without changing a single distance. For each cut we choose MMP when the sub-mesh is
large relative to the members' box, and VTP otherwise. VTP needs its per-sweep state reset and consistently
oriented faces; it links its window lists from the first edge, so it also rejects sub-meshes that fall into
several pieces, which we sweep piece by piece. On Gunnison10k the hybrid build takes 202\,s, against 284\,s
with MMP alone and 215\,s with VTP alone, with identical labels.

\paragraph{Closed-loop label coding.}
Labels are stored as float32 by default, rounded up so that they are never below the solver's double-precision
value, and lower bounds subtract the matching relative margin. Most of the index is label rows, and neighbouring entries of a
row differ by at most the arc gap between their portals (the rows are 1-Lipschitz). We therefore code a row in
runs of deltas with 16 or 8 bits, anchored by a float32 value per run, and encode in a closed loop: each code
is chosen from the decoded value of the previous entry, so the error does not accumulate. Every decoded value
is at least the stored one, so the upper bound stays an upper bound; the lower bound subtracts the bounded
decoding error, and the estimate is de-biased by half of it. A block scan over 8 portals at a time with four
accumulators keeps the decoding cost low. The near tables are stored once per pair, in the row of the smaller
vertex id, with varint id deltas; ratio-coded variants with 16 and 8 bits store
$D=|PQ|(1+c\,q)$ with the least code $c$ that is not below the true distance.

\paragraph{Parallel build.}
The top cuts spread their portals over the threads and the remaining cuts go one per thread. The labels are
bit-identical to a single-threaded build, and the peak memory is about the coded index size because rows are coded as
soon as a cut is done. A memory gate bounds the solvers alive at once. The uncapped build of the 2.5M-face TJ takes
110\,h on one thread, 24.6\,h on 8 threads ($4.5\times$), 16.0\,h on 16 ($6.9\times$) and 15.7\,h on 32. The ceiling is
not idle threads: from 16 to 32 threads the CPU time grows 2.7-fold for the same wall time, so the extra threads
most likely contend for memory bandwidth (the exact sweeps chase pointers through large window lists, and the node has two
sockets), while at 16 threads the cores are busy 72\% of the time, and we attribute the idle rest to the few large top-cut
sweeps. Query
timings always use one thread, and build timings do unless stated.

\paragraph{Certified proximity queries.}
For a kNN or range query from $s$ over a set of objects, the index gives an interval for each candidate. An
object whose $\UB$ is below the $k$-th smallest $\LB$ is in the answer, one whose $\LB$ exceeds the $k$-th
smallest $\UB$ is not, and only the ambiguous ones need an exact sweep from $s$, which we run once for all of
them on an ellipse sub-mesh. Without that sweep, returning the $k$ objects of smallest $\UB$ is a certified
$\eps$-approximate kNN: every returned object has $d\le(1+\eps)d_k$, with $d_k$ the true $k$-th distance, because
each of the true $k$ nearest objects has $\UB\le(1+\eps)d\le(1+\eps)d_k$. Likewise, the objects with $\UB\le r$ form
a range answer with no false positives that contains every object within $r/(1+\eps)$. Both need
$\UB\le(1+\eps)d$, which Theorem~\ref{thm:main} gives for the uncapped index. With the capped index, a candidate
whose interval fails the acceptance test is refined first if it could matter, that is if its $\LB$ is at most the
$k$-th smallest $\UB$ (kNN) or at most $r/(1+\eps)$ (range); then $\UB\le(1+\eps)\LB\le(1+\eps)d$ or the distance
is exact for every object the guarantee needs. Our proximity experiments use the uncapped index.

\paragraph{Weighted terrains.}
Exact weighted geodesics have no practical algorithm, so on weighted terrains the labels are distances in a
Steiner graph with $m$ points per edge, shared by all searches and searched with a stop once all members of a
cut are settled. Graph paths are curves on the surface, so $\UB$ is still the cost of an actual path, but the
labels are not exact, so the interval lower bound no longer holds and we keep only the chord bound
$\lambda|S-T|$. The weighted index therefore returns an upper bound and an estimate, not a $(1+\eps)$
certificate, and the theorems of Section~\ref{sec:index} apply to it only if exact weighted labels become
available. Our weighted experiments are exploratory in this sense.

\paragraph{Obstacles.}
An obstacle removes the faces whose chart centroid lies inside it, so the surface gets a hole and paths go
around it. The certificate needs no change. A hole only lengthens paths, so every chart lower bound still
holds; $\mu_C$ is a graph distance on the holed mesh, so a detour widens the window by itself; a cut line
broken by holes gives several runs, each bracketed by portals at its ends; and the near tables hold every pair
within chart radius $R$ with its exact distance, including pairs close in the chart but separated by a wall.
One practical point matters. Where two holes touch at a single vertex the mesh is no longer a manifold, and
there Kirsanov's MMP returned distances up to 5\% too long, longer than a path in a Steiner graph. We
therefore separate touching holes by keeping the largest fan of faces at such vertices; on the cleaned obstacle
meshes of Gunnison10k MMP and VTP agree to $10^{-15}$.

\paragraph{Two defects in VTP.}
On the cluster meshes, VTP as released returned distances up to 3\% too long at a few vertices, longer than a path
in a Steiner graph, while MMP never did. There are two causes, in its vertex queue and in a window-pruning rule. The queue is a
\texttt{std::set} ordered by distance alone, so a vertex at exactly the distance of another queued vertex is
silently dropped, or the wrong one is erased; such a vertex is never popped and never becomes a pseudo-source, and
everything in its shadow gets a too-long distance. Ties are common on regular grids with integer heights, and rare
on irregular meshes such as GunnisonForest, which is why earlier checks agreed. Ordering the queue by distance and
vertex id, and removing a vertex before changing its key, fixes the queue. The remaining errors, up to
$10^{-5}$, come from the rule that drops one of two windows on an edge where their rays cross: it assumes the
crossing lies on the surface, but each window is unfolded through its own chain of triangles, so the crossing may
not exist. We no longer apply that rule. After both changes, MMP and VTP agree to $2\cdot10^{-11}$ or better on all 200 sources we checked
on 28 meshes, and a VTP sweep takes 1.1--1.4 times as long as before on small meshes and 1.5--1.7 times at 0.5--2.5M faces. All results in
Section~\ref{sec:server} use the fixed VTP; the ground truth is MMP.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Setup.}
The experiments on the small terrains run on an Apple M1 laptop (8\,GB), and those on terrains with
0.5--5M faces on an HPC cluster (Section~\ref{sec:server}); every method runs on one thread. Our index is
implemented in C++17. The ground truth is exact MMP in double precision. We use the four benchmark terrains of the
SE-Oracle and EAR-Oracle papers (HorseMount, BigMount, HeadlightMount, RobinsonMountain, 793--3,696
vertices), Gunnison10k (9,610 vertices) and a 100k-vertex DEM, GunnisonForest100k. Our index and DGG answer the
same 1,500 random vertex pairs per terrain (seed 7). EAR-Oracle and the other methods of its authors' harness
answer 100 pairs with the points moved 5\,cm into a face, because the harness cannot locate a point exactly at a
vertex. The portal gap $g$ is given in mean edge lengths, and we report $\eps$ and $g$ per experiment.

\paragraph{Competitors.}
Every competitor runs with its authors' code: SE-Oracle, EAR-Oracle, K-Algo and the on-the-fly Steiner
schemes in the harness of the EAR-Oracle authors~\cite{huang2023ear}, with a fix for weighted endpoints that
changes their published weighted errors; UP-Oracle, WSPD-Oracle and RC-Oracle from the UP-Oracle
code~\cite{yan2025up}; DGG~\cite{adikusuma2020fast} with a port to our platform; NeuroGF~\cite{zhang2023neurogf}
with the authors' network and training, trained on exact distances and paths of our terrains; and exact MMP and
VTP on the fly.

\paragraph{Query time.}
\cci answers in 0.18--0.36\,$\mu$s, the mean of the best of five passes over the query set. Repeated passes keep
the labels a query set touches in cache, so we also timed one pass over 100,000 independent random pairs on
Gunnison10k: the mean is 0.51\,$\mu$s instead of 0.32\,$\mu$s, the median 0.42\,$\mu$s and the 99th percentile
0.96\,$\mu$s. EAR-Oracle takes 1.3--10.2\,ms, so we are 7,000--28,000 times faster, and
DGG, which runs Dijkstra on its graph, takes 1.4--22\,ms. An exact VTP sweep per query costs 3.1--59\,ms, and
K-Algo and the on-the-fly Steiner schemes are slower still (Figure~\ref{fig:methods}).

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
With $\eps=0.1$ our mean error is 0.0076--0.024\% and the maximum 0.37--1.79\%, while EAR-Oracle has a mean
error of 3.1--3.5\% and a maximum of 6.4--24.5\%. Its nominal $\eps$ is relative to its Steiner graph, and the
Steiner graph with 5 points per edge is itself about 3\% longer than the surface: on the same 100 pairs, an
exact search on that Steiner graph (the harness's FixedS) has the same mean error, 3.2\% on BigMount, so a smaller
$\eps$ than its default of 0.2 would not help. Increasing the number of
Steiner points to 10 and 20 on HorseMount lowers EAR-Oracle's mean error only to 2.5\% and 2.3\%, at 541 and
683\,MB. SE-Oracle reaches 0.81\% on HorseMount but already needs 1.7\,h of build and 942\,MB
at its 793 vertices, so on the larger terrains we compare against EAR-Oracle, its successor from the same group. DGG is the most accurate
approximate method, with mean errors of about 0.0025\%, but its queries are four orders of magnitude slower
than ours and return no per-query bound.

\paragraph{Certificates.}
All 7,500 answers in Table~\ref{tab:main} are certified and correct. The guaranteed bound is never violated,
and it is about two times conservative in the worst case: with $\eps=0.1$ the largest $\UB$ error over all
pairs is 0.9--3.4\%, against the guaranteed 10\%.

\paragraph{Size and build.}
The index is 1.3--37\,MB, 19--145 times smaller than EAR-Oracle's, and builds 3--8 times faster.
DGG's graph is two to three times larger than our index and builds faster.

\subsection{Proximity queries}

\subsection{The POI setting}

UP-Oracle and its baselines answer queries between a fixed set of points of interest. In that setting the simplest
exact index is a $P\times P$ table built by one VTP sweep per POI. It is exact, sub-microsecond, and the cheapest
to build for up to about 1,000 POIs on our terrains, which neither UP-Oracle nor our index beats. Our index in POI
mode, which indexes only the POIs, becomes smaller than the table from about 1,000 POIs, and at 3,000 POIs on
Gunnison10k it takes 3.1\,MB against the table's 36\,MB. Our index is designed for queries between all vertices and
arbitrary points, where no table fits.

\paragraph{Main comparison at 0.5M faces.}
Tables~\ref{tab:server-ours} and~\ref{tab:server-comp} and Figure~\ref{fig:server-methods} give the results.
Uncapped, our index answers in 1.0--1.9\,$\mu$s with a mean error of 0.0011--0.0016\% and a maximum of 0.15--0.30\%,
in 1.9--2.1\,GB of float32 labels or 0.60--0.66\,GB of 8-bit codes, built in 10--19\,h on one thread. With a cap of
64 portals per cut it takes 355--366\,MB, answers in 0.5--1.5\,$\mu$s with a mean error of 0.014--0.029\%, and builds in
7--11\,h; on TJ and GI the exact refine was needed for none of the random pairs, and for one pair each over all
query sets. EAR-Oracle with its default of 5 Steiner points per edge needs
333\,GB and 17--21\,h, answers in 0.4--1.0\,s, and has a mean error of 2.6--2.9\% and a maximum of 8.5--9.8\%. With 7
points it needs 468\,GB and 35\,h and still has 1.9\%. Its peak memory follows from its design: every leaf box stores
the distances between its boundary vertices and all Steiner points inside it, both ways, in a balanced tree, which
grows like the number of Steiner points times $n^{1.5}$ for a fixed number of boxes. On the authors' own
GunnisonForest (100,511 vertices) it needs 93\,GB and 5.4\,h, with a mean error of 2.9\%; our index on the same
terrain takes 480\,MB, 1.0\,h and 0.0077\%. So our index is 540--930 times smaller, answers five to six orders of magnitude faster, and its mean error is
three orders of magnitude lower.

DGG is the strongest competitor in accuracy and build time: its graph builds in minutes, and with $\eps=10^{-4}$ its
mean error is 0.004\%, comparable to ours. But a DGG query is a Dijkstra search on a graph of 0.8--2.4\,GB. The full
search of the authors' code takes 0.26--1.1\,s on the six terrains, and stopping at the target 0.12--0.13\,s with
$\eps=10^{-3}$ and 0.29--0.51\,s with $10^{-4}$. It returns no per-query bound. At the same size our index is more accurate: with 8-bit labels the
uncapped index takes 0.62\,GB on TJ for a mean error of 0.0021\%, against DGG's 0.0037\% at 2.4\,GB
(Figure~\ref{fig:server-methods}).

Could a road-network index on DGG's graph close the gap? We tried the two standard ones, on TJ and GI. A
contraction hierarchy (edge-difference order, witness searches, a core once the mean degree has tripled) stalls: the
graph has 94 out-edges per vertex with $\eps=10^{-3}$ and 295 with $10^{-4}$, and every contraction adds shortcuts
among neighbours that are rarely witnessed. With $\eps=10^{-3}$ it contracts 43\% of the vertices in 26--31\,h before
the core is reached, with $10^{-4}$ only 13\% in 40\,h, and the queries, upward searches into a core of 142,000--219,000
vertices, are 1.3--1.5 times \emph{slower} than the stopped Dijkstra (6.7 times on one terrain). Hub labels (pruned landmark labelling in degree order, exact
on the graph) do complete: with $\eps=10^{-3}$ they hold 14,000--15,000 entries per vertex, 29--30\,GB, built in
13--15\,h with a peak of 82--84\,GB, and with $10^{-4}$ 23,000--24,000 entries, 46--48\,GB, in 38--42\,h and up to
141\,GB. They answer in 58--94\,$\mu$s with DGG's accuracy. Against them our uncapped index with 8-bit labels is 48--77 times
smaller, builds faster (11.7--11.8\,h), answers 30--60 times faster, is more accurate than even the $\eps=10^{-4}$
graph (0.0021--0.0025\% against 0.0035--0.0037\%), and certifies every answer. So the gap is not the missing index: DGG's graph has more than 90 edges per vertex, and
labels on it grow with that density, while our labels are distances on the surface itself, sampled only where a
shortest path can cross a cut.
We see DGG and our index as answering different needs:
DGG is the better choice for a few thousand queries on a terrain that will not be queried again, ours when the terrain is
queried many times, needs answers in microseconds, or needs a bound on each answer.
NeuroGF is the smallest index by far, 0.25\,MB of network weights, but its mean error is 0.2--0.6\% and single
pairs are off by 4--13\%, with no indication of which. We checked that this is not an artefact of our setup.
Distance-to-surface targets measured exactly instead of against a subsampled surface give the same accuracy (0.41\%
against 0.40\% on TJ), and 800 epochs instead of their 400 lower the mean error by 11--15\% and the maximum from 13.4\%
to 9.1\%, at twice the training time. An exact VTP sweep takes 6.3--6.6\,s per query.
SEP~\cite{gotsman2022compressing}, which we reimplemented with exact VTP sweeps, builds in 2.7--3.1\,h into
228--236\,MB (110--114 doubles per vertex) and answers in 6.5--8.7\,$\mu$s with a mean error of 0.34--0.37\%, close to
the 0.1--0.4\% its authors report on meshes of up to 5k vertices. It builds faster than our index and is smaller than
any index but NeuroGF's, but its errors go both ways, from $-2.8\%$ to $+2.4\%$, and nothing in an answer says which
pairs are off. At about its size our index is more accurate and faster: with $\eps=0.8$, $g=4$ and 8-bit labels it
takes 265\,MB, builds in 3.3--3.6\,h and answers in 0.6\,$\mu$s with a mean error of 0.016--0.020\%, about 20 times lower, and

\paragraph{Exact-landmark intervals.}
The simplest certified alternative stores, for every vertex, its exact distances to $L$ landmarks and returns the
triangle-inequality interval through them, as Burghard and Klein do on meshes~\cite{burghard2015simple} and ALT
does on road networks~\cite{goldberg2005computing}. Built with one exact sweep per landmark, it is fast to build
and tight for far pairs (Table~\ref{tab:landmarks}): with $L=256$ (257\,MB, under half an hour) the median width on
the random pairs is 0.07--0.09\%, against 2.0\% for our cap-64 index of similar size, and with $L=1{,}024$ it is
0.01\%. But its width is not bounded, and it fails exactly where a proximity query needs it. For close pairs
neither bound is useful: the upper bound goes through a landmark that is far from both points, and the lower
bound sees two nearly equal distances. On the near pairs the median width is 620--640\% with 256 landmarks and
260--270\% with 1,024, fewer than 4\% of them are certified at $1+\eps=1.2$, and the upper bound can be 23--50 times
the distance; with 256 landmarks the median width falls below 2\% only from 8--16 near radii on. Over all vertex pairs, 54--60\% are certified, against
99.98--100\% for ours, so the landmark index has to refine about half of a mixed workload, at 15--80\,ms per exact
sweep, and 98--100\% of its kNN queries (see below). Our index costs more to build because it buys the bound
everywhere: near pairs are exact, and every interval has $\UB\le(1+\eps)\LB$ or is refined.

\paragraph{When the build pays off.}
Our build takes hours where DGG's takes minutes, but it is amortised. With cap 64 at 0.5M faces
it pays for itself after 3,700--4,500 queries compared with answering every query by an exact VTP sweep, and after
51,000--127,000 queries compared with DGG's stopped search ($\eps=10^{-4}$, including DGG's own build; 23,000--58,000
against its full search); the uncapped index after 6,400--7,100 and 88,000--215,000 queries. At 2.5M faces, where we
have only DGG's full search, the capped index breaks even after 7,800--8,100 queries
against VTP and 49,000 against DGG; the uncapped index built on one thread (110--125\,h) after 16,600--19,600 and
106,000, and built on 32 threads (15.7\,h) after 2,400 and 14,800. At 5M faces, where a VTP sweep takes 74\,s and a
full DGG query 6.9\,s, the capped index built on 32 threads (12.0\,h) breaks even after 590 and 5,700 queries; if the
stopped search is 2.2 times faster there as at 0.5M, these counts against DGG roughly double.
Against EAR-Oracle there is no break-even point: its build is longer and each of its queries slower. A single kNN
query over 10,000 objects asks for up to 10,000 distances, so one batch of proximity queries, or the queries of a
route planner over a day, already exceeds these counts.

\paragraph{Error by distance.}
On TJ, pairs closer than the near radius are answered exactly from the near tables. The error peaks just beyond
the near radius, with a mean of 0.13\% and a maximum of 1.8\% in the first distance band, and falls with distance
to a maximum of 0.0007\% in the last band: the window lemma leaves the most slack where the two points are close to the cut.

\paragraph{Arbitrary points and weights.}
A2A queries between arbitrary surface points take 10--18\,$\mu$s uncapped, with a mean error of 0.002--0.006\%, and
4--7\,$\mu$s with cap 64 at 0.016--0.037\%, all certified. EAR-Oracle has a mean error of 2.6\% and 2.9\% on the
same A2A pairs of TJ and GI, at 237 and 389\,ms per query, and NeuroGF 0.37\% on TJ. On the weighted versions of TJ
and GI, with the face weights of the EAR-Oracle benchmark, our index with 5 Steiner points per edge differs from a
reference Steiner graph with 12 points by 0.10\% on average (maximum 0.6--0.9\%), takes 1.6\,GB with 8-bit labels,
builds in 9.8\,h and answers in 2.1--2.2\,$\mu$s. EAR-Oracle differs from the same reference by 1.5--1.8\%, with
333\,GB, 23\,h and 0.35--0.63\,s per query. For many pairs our upper bound is shorter than the reference, so the
reference is not exact and these are differences rather than errors; as in Section~\ref{sec:engineering}, the
weighted index certifies only the upper bound.

\paragraph{Scaling to 5M faces.}
Table~\ref{tab:scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} follow TJ and GI from 0.5M to 2.5M faces (1.25M vertices), and TJ
further to 5M faces. The uncapped index stores labels growing like $n^{1.48}$, the $\Theta(n^{1.5})$ of
Table~\ref{tab:complexity}, and reaches 23\,GB at 1.25M vertices; its build grows like $n^{1.45}$--$n^{1.47}$ and takes 96--110\,h
on one thread at 1M vertices and 110--125\,h at 1.25M vertices. With cap 64 the labels grow like $n^{1.14}$, the
index takes 6.0\,GB (2.1\,GB with 8-bit codes) at 1.25M vertices, and the build grows like $n^{1.2}$ and
takes 51--52\,h. The query time stays at 0.3--1.2\,$\mu$s capped and grows to 3.0--3.3\,$\mu$s at 1M vertices
uncapped, while the mean error does not grow: it falls from 0.0012--0.0016\% to 0.0007--0.0008\% uncapped and stays
at 0.019--0.027\% capped. All answers at every size are certified and correct. The parallel build of the uncapped
index on the 2.5M-face TJ takes 24.6, 16.0 and 15.7\,h with 8, 16 and 32 threads, with identical labels, against
110\,h on one; beyond 16 threads the speed-up flattens at $7\times$ because of memory contention
(Section~\ref{sec:engineering}).

To see whether the capped index keeps scaling, we resampled the 2.5M-face TJ grid to 5M faces (2.51M vertices) on
the same surface, adding no relief. This tests how the index grows with the number of vertices at a fixed relief,
not how it behaves on a finer real terrain, which we do not have at this resolution. Built on 32 threads in 12.0\,h, the capped index takes 12.9\,GB, or 4.7\,GB with
8-bit codes, 2.15 times its size at 2.5M faces, in line with $n^{1.1}$. It answers in 0.34\,$\mu$s (0.52\,$\mu$s
with 8-bit codes) with a mean error of 0.019\% and a maximum of 1.3\%, without a single exact refine, and all
answers, over every query set, are certified and correct. On this terrain DGG with $\eps=10^{-4}$ builds a 23.6\,GB
graph in 1.1\,h and answers in 6.9\,s, and an exact VTP sweep takes 74\,s. The shifted hierarchy, 1.4 times larger than the kd
hierarchy at 100k vertices, is smaller from 1M faces on (4.5 against 5.5\,GB at 1M, 6.8 against 9.4\,GB at 1.5M),
the crossover its constants predict, but its mean error is 0.08--0.09\% and it builds more slowly, so cap 64
(2.1 and 3.3\,GB at 0.02\%) dominates it. DGG's graph grows linearly and is smaller than our uncapped index from 1M faces, but larger than
the capped one at every size, and its queries slow down from 0.8 to 3.7\,s. From its memory growth EAR-Oracle would need about
0.9\,TB at 1M faces, more than the 768\,GB of a node.

\paragraph{Ablations.}
On TJ and GI at 0.5M faces the cap trades size for error smoothly: caps of 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 and 256 portals give
413\,MB to 1.70\,GB (float32), with mean errors from 0.55--0.64\% down to 0.002\%. The exact refine runs for
128--137, 34--36, 9--12, 1 and 0 of the 6,621 pairs with caps 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128. With cap 8 it runs for
1.0--1.3\% of the random pairs at 1.1--1.7\,s each, so the end-to-end mean is 11--22\,ms against a 0.37\,$\mu$s
lookup, a cost the 99th percentile hides on TJ (12\,$\mu$s); with cap 64 (8-bit) it is 0.60 and 1.54\,$\mu$s on
TJ and GI, against lookups of 0.55 and 1.54\,$\mu$s. Thorup-style $\eps_c$-covers are dominated:
with $\eps_c=0.05$ they take 483\,MB for a mean error of 0.82\%, worse than cap 8 at 413\,MB, and with
$\eps_c=0.01$ 1.13\,GB for 0.082\%, against 0.0021\% for the uncapped 8-bit index at 0.62\,GB. For the label
sweeps, MMP alone takes 19--24\,h with 3\,GB of memory, and VTP alone 18--27\,h with 300\,GB. The hybrid takes 13--18\,h with
30\,GB, and 18\,h with 75\,GB when it prefers VTP more often, so it is 1.4--1.5 times faster than either solver alone
with a tenth of VTP's memory.

\paragraph{Adversarial terrains.}
The capped theorem holds under bounded slope and detour, and its terms grow with the slope ($\lambda$ falls), the
window ratio $\kappa_C$ and the number of runs $r_C$ in which a shortest path crosses a cut. To see how the index
degrades when these grow, we built it on seven variants of TJ at 0.5M faces (Table~\ref{tab:adversarial}):
heights scaled by 5 and 10 (median slopes of 63$^\circ$ and 76$^\circ$ instead of 22$^\circ$), the chart stretched by 10
in $x$ (every triangle a sliver of aspect 10), needle triangles (alternating grid columns 0.05 and 1.95 apart,
aspect up to 43), heights rounded to 5\,m (129 distinct heights, many exact ties between paths), 90\% of the points
in 20 Gaussian clusters with 200 near-coincident pairs $10^{-4}$ apart (edges from $10^{-3}$ to 5,000), and 40 thin
walls that alternate between the two sides of the terrain, so that a shortest path across it zig-zags through a
comb and crosses many cuts several times.

Every answer stays certified and correct, and the accuracy barely moves: the uncapped mean error is
0.0012--0.0040\% on every variant, against 0.0012\% on TJ. What the adversarial geometry costs is the build and
the size, as the theorem predicts. With heights $\times5$, the windows and ellipses grow with $1/\lambda$: the
uncapped index stores 1.76 times as many labels (3.4\,GB) and builds in 27.8\,h instead of 11.7\,h, and the capped
index builds in 20\,h, keeps its size bounded by the cap (0.44\,GB), and its mean error (8-bit) rises from 0.021\% to
0.069\%, with 8 refines over the 6,621 pairs of all query sets. Slivers and needles do not enlarge the index or its errors
and lengthen the build by at most 1.4 times; ties make it faster (more coplanar faces for the exact sweeps). DGG keeps its accuracy on every variant, at 0.09--0.44\,s per query.

On \emph{cluster} the exact MMP sweeps themselves fail. Its degenerate faces (aspect ratios up to 30,000) make MMP
miss one or two vertices from a typical source and return distances up to $7\cdot10^{-4}$ too long at a few others,
which VTP, the other exact solver, does not show. Two answers of the capped index fall outside the MMP reference by
more than the tolerance, and they are the only ones in all our runs that do: a target MMP never reached, for which
VTP gives 4,452.04 and our upper bound is 4,452.4, and a pair for which MMP is $3.2\cdot10^{-4}$ too long, by more than
the $1.05\cdot10^{-4}$ our upper bound lies below it. We leave the unreached pair out of the error statistics of this
row. Because our labels on this terrain are MMP sweeps too, a clean test needs VTP for both labels and reference; we
report that run, which is in progress, separately.

\paragraph{kNN and range queries in the POI setting.}
UP-Oracle's own workload asks for distances between points of interest, and we ran kNN ($k=1,10,25$) and range
queries (radius 1, 3 and 10\% of the chart diagonal) from 20 of them over all others, with their POI files of 50,
500 and 2,500 points on the six terrains at 0.5M faces. The UP-Oracle family has no proximity operator, so it
answers every query-object pair. Our index over the POIs takes 1.2, 2.4--2.6 and 6.7--7.3\,MB and builds in
4--5\,h. Its certified-approximate kNN with $k=10$ takes 29--73\,$\mu$s with recall at least 99.5\%, and range
queries 2--93\,$\mu$s. UP-Oracle takes 0.7--0.8\,ms per kNN query with 50 POIs, 50--72\,ms with 500 and 97--122\,s
with 2,500, where its index is 2.1--2.4\,GB and builds in 19--25\,h; WSPD-Oracle and RC-Oracle are faster at 2,500
POIs (2.6--5.9\,s) but slower at 50 and 500. DGG with an early-stopping Dijkstra takes 20--26\,ms at 50 POIs and
0.14--0.20\,ms at 2,500. Our exact mode needs sweeps when the POIs are sparse: 0.1--0.9\,s at 50 POIs,
0.7--8.9\,ms at 500 and 0.06--0.5\,ms at 2,500. In the plain POI setting, distances between POIs only, our POI index
answers in 0.7--0.9\,$\mu$s with a mean error of 0.003--0.008\%, against UP-Oracle's 0.4--2.3\% at 15\,$\mu$s to
57\,ms; but the exact $P\times P$ table built with one VTP sweep per POI stays the best choice up to 2,500 POIs
(25\,MB, 0.04--3\,h), as on the small terrains.

\paragraph{Proximity over many objects.}
With 10,000 random objects at 0.5M faces, certified-approximate kNN answers in 23--155\,$\mu$s for $k=1$ and
60--169\,$\mu$s for $k=10$ with recall of at least 98.9\%. The exact mode needs a sweep only for the candidates the
intervals cannot separate, in 0--2\% of the queries for $k=1$ and 13--22\% for $k=10$ (0.3--0.5 objects per query on
average): 24--158\,$\mu$s for $k=1$ and 0.3--0.5\,ms for $k=10$. An on-the-fly search from the query point that stops
once the $k$ nearest objects are settled, pruned by chart distances, takes 1.2--1.9\,ms and 3.8--7.0\,ms, and a full
sweep 2.7--3.2\,s. At 2.5M faces, $k=10$ takes 0.4\,ms approximately and 2.5\,ms exactly, against an 11\,s sweep.
DGG's Dijkstra stopped once $k$ objects are settled is the strongest uncertified alternative. With
$\eps=10^{-3}$ it answers $k=1$, 10 and 100 in 0.03, 0.19--0.23 and 1.9--2.2\,ms with a recall of at least 99.8\%,
and range queries of radius 1, 3 and 10\% of the diagonal in 0.12, 1.0 and 10.5--11.5\,ms (2.2--2.6 times as long
with $10^{-4}$). Our certified-approximate mode is faster except for $k=1$: 60--169\,$\mu$s for $k=10$, 0.4--2.0\,ms
for $k=100$, and 0.9--1.2\,ms for the 10\% range, 9--12 times faster than DGG, and each answer is proved: $d\le(1+\eps)d_k$
for every returned neighbour, and no false positive and no miss within $r/(1+\eps)$ in a range. Our exact mode is the slow one when the answer is large. It refines the
undecided objects with one sweep, which takes 32--54\,ms for $k=100$ and 0.5\,s for the 10\% range, where DGG's
recall is 99.9\% but unproved. So for exact answers over many objects per query a graph search is the better tool,
and the index pays where each answer must carry a bound that $\eps$ satisfies, or where many queries each ask
for a few objects. Landmark intervals (Table~\ref{tab:landmarks}) decide kNN by the same rules as ours, but their intervals are too loose
for it: with 256 landmarks, 57 objects per query remain undecided for $k=10$ and every query needs a sweep, 28\,ms
end to end, and with 1,024 landmarks 23 objects and 41\,ms, since computing the 10,000 intervals alone takes 35\,ms.

\paragraph{The small terrains on the cluster.}
Rerunning the small-terrain configurations of Table~\ref{tab:main} on the cluster gives the same picture: on
Gunnison10k with $\eps=0.1$ the build takes 194\,s (202\,s on the laptop) and the mean error is 0.0076\%.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments support the main claim: a terrain distance oracle can answer in about a microsecond and
certify every answer, with errors far below those of the Steiner-graph oracles and an index two to three orders
of magnitude smaller, up to terrains with millions of faces. The certificate is what makes the index usable as a database operator. A kNN or range query can
decide most candidates from the intervals alone, and a user who needs a guaranteed answer can ask for $\UB$, which is
the length of an actual path, or for an exact refinement only when the interval is too wide. DGG reaches a similar
accuracy with a much shorter build, but searches per query. Table~\ref{tab:guide} sums up which method to use when, from our measurements.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Asymptotic size.}
The uncapped index stores $\Theta(n^{1.5})$ labels, as the top cuts have $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ portals and every vertex
stores them. The capped variant is near-linear only for bounded slope, detour and number of runs, its accuracy
relies on an exact refine. Theorem~\ref{thm:locality} confines it to pairs close to a capped cut and makes it a
local sweep, but bounds the number of such pairs only under density assumptions, and loosely. Djidjev and Sommer's
oracle is asymptotically smaller in theory. In floating point the guarantees are validated, not proved
(Section~\ref{sec:index}).

\paragraph{Build.}
The build is dominated by one exact sweep per portal. Uncapped it grows like $n^{1.5}$: at 2.5M faces it takes
16\,h on 16 threads and 110--125\,h on one; capped it takes 51--52\,h on one thread at 2.5M faces, and 12\,h on 32
threads at 5M faces. The parallel build stops scaling at about 16 threads. Paths that detour around walls make it 2.5 times longer. The build is the price of the certificate and of microsecond queries, and it is recovered after roughly
600--215,000 queries, depending on the terrain size, the variant and the alternative.

\paragraph{Slopes and weights.}
$\lambda$ enters the near radius, the window and the ellipse, and a small $w_{\min}$ widens all three. Steep
slopes raise $\sigma_C$ and lengthen the capped tails: with heights scaled by 5 (median slope 63$^\circ$) the
uncapped index stores 1.8 times as many labels and builds 2.4 times as long, though its answers stay as accurate
(Table~\ref{tab:adversarial}). On weighted terrains we certify only the upper bound.

\paragraph{Distances, not paths.}
The index returns distances and intervals, not paths. $\UB$ is the length of a path through the minimising portal,
and that path could be traced by two exact sweeps from $s$ and $t$ on their ellipse sub-meshes, but we have not
implemented path reporting, and all our timings are for distances.

\paragraph{Static terrains.}
The index is static. Terrains that change, the setting of UP-Oracle, would need the affected cuts rebuilt.

\section{Conclusion}

The Certified Cut Index answers terrain distance queries in about a microsecond with an interval within a factor
$1\pm\eps$ of the exact surface distance, so kNN and range queries are decided from the intervals and refined only
where needed. Future work includes validated enclosures for the solvers, a faster build, and updates for changing
terrains.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

We use the notation of Section~\ref{sec:index}. In the implementation a portal that falls within $10^{-7}$ of a face
edge is moved along the line by about $10^{-5}$ edge lengths before it is inserted, so a run end may not be a portal.
It then leaves an end stretch $[\text{run end},p_k]$ of arc $e_k$, and the lower bound also takes the term $f_k-2e_k$.

\begin{lemma}[crossing]
Every curve on $T$ between two points on opposite sides of $\ell_C$ meets $\ell_C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The $a$-coordinate of its chart projection is continuous, below $v$ at one end and above at the other.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich} with end stretches]
If the crossing $x^\ast$ lies in an end stretch with portal $p_k$, then $f_k\le d(s,x^\ast)+2A(x^\ast,p_k)+d(x^\ast,t)
\le d(s,t)+2e_k$, so $f_k-2e_k\le d(s,t)$. The interval case is as in the main text.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Let $|s-t|>R$ and $d=d(s,t)$, so $d\ge\lambda|s-t|>g/\eps$ by~\eqref{eq:lower}. Let $x^\ast$ be the crossing of a
shortest path in the interval $[p_j,p_{j+1}]$ with arc gap $A_j$, and $a=A(p_j,x^\ast)$.

\emph{Upper bound.} $\min(f_j,f_{j+1})\le d+\min(2a,2(A_j-a))\le d+A_j$. If the interval lies in $I_C$ then
$A_j\le g<\eps d$. Otherwise every point of the interval, $x^\ast$ included, has offset at least $c_j$ from $I_C$, so
$d\ge 2\lambda c_j$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:window}, and~\eqref{eq:gaps} gives $A_j\le\max(g,2\eps\lambda c_j)\le\eps d$. In
an end stretch, $f_k\le d+2e_k$, and $2e_k$ is far below $g<\eps d$.

\emph{Lower bound.} Every $f\ge d$, so an interval term satisfies $\frac12(f_j+f_{j+1})-A_j\ge d-A_j$, which is at
least $(1-\eps)d$ if $A_j\le\eps d$. Otherwise $A_j>\eps d>g$, so the interval is a tail interval with
$A_j\le 2\eps\lambda c_j$, hence $c_j>d/(2\lambda)$. Both of its portals $p$ have offset at least $c_j$ from $B_M$,
so by~\eqref{eq:lower} $f(p)\ge\lambda(|s-p|+|p-t|)\ge 2\lambda c_j$, and the term is at least
$2\lambda c_j(1-\eps)>(1-\eps)d$. An end-stretch term is $f_k-2e_k\ge d-2e_k>(1-\eps)d$. The chord term is a lower
bound, so $\LB\ge(1-\eps)d$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:capped}]
Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich} holds for any portal set, so $\LB\le d\le\UB$. The query accepts an answer only if
$\UB\le(1+\eps)\LB$, and computes every other pair exactly. A refined
pair is computed by one exact sweep on the faces meeting $\mathrm{bbox}(s,t)$ dilated by $\UB/(2\lambda)$, which
contains every path of length at most $\UB$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:ellipse}, so it is exact.

For the size, a run whose part inside $I_C$ has cost $a$ gets at most $a/g_C+2$ portals there, plus its two ends,
and $g_C\ge A(I_C)/k$, so the box holds at most $k+O(r_C)$ portals. Cost and chart length along the cut satisfy
$\lambda\,dc\le dA\le\sigma_C\,dc$. In a tail the gap is $g_C$ while $2\eps\lambda c<g_C$, a chart length of
$g_C/(2\eps\lambda)$ and so at most $\sigma_C/(2\eps\lambda)+O(r_C)$ portals. From then on a step of cost $2\eps\lambda c$
advances the chart offset by at least $2\eps\lambda c/\sigma_C$, so the offset grows by a factor at least
$1+2\eps\lambda/\sigma_C$ per portal, from $g_C/(2\eps\lambda)$ up to the window length $\mu_C/\lambda$. Since
$g_C\ge A(I_C)/k\ge\lambda|I_C|/k$, the ratio of the two offsets is at most $2\eps\mu_C/g_C\le2\eps k\kappa_C$, and as
$2\eps\lambda/\sigma_C\le2$, this takes at most $\frac{\sigma_C}{\eps\lambda}\log^+(2\eps k\kappa_C)+O(r_C)$ portals.
A vertex stores the rows of the cuts on its path to the root, which gives the label count, and its near table
holds $|N_R(u)|$ entries. If $\sigma_C/\lambda$, $\kappa_C$ and $r_C$ are bounded, a cut has $k+O(\eps^{-1}\log k)$
portals, and median cuts give a hierarchy of depth $O(\log n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:locality}]
Let $d=d(s,t)$ and $m=\max(g_C,\delta d)$. \emph{Upper bound:} as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main},
$\UB\le d+A_j$ for the interval holding a crossing $x^\ast$. Inside $I_C$, $A_j\le g_C$; in a tail, $x^\ast$ has offset
at least $c_j$, so $d\ge2\lambda c_j$ and $A_j\le\max(g_C,2\delta\lambda c_j)\le m$. \emph{Lower bound:} an interval
term with $A_j\le g_C$ is at least $d-g_C$. Any other is a tail term with $A_j\le2\delta\lambda c_j$ whose portals
have offset at least $c_j$ from $B_M$, so $f\ge\max(d,2\lambda c_j)$ at both, and the term is at least
$\max(d,2\lambda c_j)(1-\delta)\ge(1-\delta)d$. End-stretch terms are at least $d-2e_k$ with $2e_k<g$. So
$\LB\ge d-m$. If $d\ge g_C/\delta$ then $m=\delta d$ and $\UB/\LB\le(1+\delta)/(1-\delta)=1+\eps$, so the pair is
accepted. For a refined pair, $\lambda|S-T|\le d<g_C/\delta$, so $|S-T|<w_C$, and since $s$ and $t$ lie on opposite
sides of the cut line, both are within $w_C$ of it. The sweep box is $\mathrm{bbox}(s,t)$ dilated by
$\UB/(2\lambda)$, and $\UB\le d+g_C<(1+\delta)g_C/\delta$, so its side is below $|S-T|+\UB/\lambda<(2+\delta)w_C$.

For the count, fix $C$ with box $B_M$ and $b_C=|I_C|$. A refined pair has an endpoint in the strip of width $w_C$
along the cut, at most $\rho_{\max}w_Cb_C$ vertices, and the other within $w_C$ of it on the other side, at most
$\rho_{\max}\pi w_C^2/2$, so $C$ refines at most $\frac\pi2\rho_{\max}^2b_Cw_C^3$ pairs. On a capped cut
$g_C=A(I_C)/k\le\sigma_Cb_C/k$, so $w_C\le\beta b_C$, and the count is at most $\frac\pi2\rho_{\max}^2\beta^3b_C^4$.
(On an uncapped cut $w_C=g/(\delta\lambda)=(2+\eps)R$, which is the second statement.) The cut crosses the
shorter side of its box, so $b_C^2\le\mathrm{area}(B_M)\le|M|/\rho_{\min}$, and the boxes at one depth $\ell$ have
disjoint interiors, so $\sum_{C\text{ at depth }\ell}b_C^4\le\mathcal A\,n/(2^\ell\rho_{\min})$ with median cuts,
where $\mathcal A\le n/\rho_{\min}$ is the area of the chart. Summing over depths gives
$\sum_Cb_C^4\le2n^2/\rho_{\min}^2$, and the bound follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:a2a}]
$\UB(p,q)$ is the cost of a path from $p$ to a corner, then a path certified by $\UB(a_i,b_j)$, then to $q$. For the
lower bound, $d(a_i,b_j)\le d(a_i,p)+d(p,q)+d(q,b_j)\le w_f|P-A_i|+d(p,q)+w_{f'}|Q-B_j|$, so every term of the
maximum is at most $d(p,q)$. Pairs whose interval is wider than $1+2\eps$, and near pairs, are computed exactly on an
ellipse sub-mesh with both points inserted as vertices.
\end{proof}
